<Step id="S1">
Enrichment: nothing waits on this lesson. An aimed ii–V made a chord home for one bar. Push that idea far enough and home itself keeps moving: here eight bars by the trio change key three times, at a brisk swing.

<Example id="hook" views="code,roll" autoplay />

<OnPass>A new home arrives every other bar, each far from the last, yet the tenor moves only by small steps: each time, a half step down takes it into the new key. Without the tenor the shifts sound even bolder.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S2">
Where do the three homes sit? Here they are alone.

<Example id="homes" views="code,roll" />

<OnPass>B, G, E♭, B: four half steps down each time, three equal steps to the octave. Put each new home's V7 in front of it and you have the eight bars of the first step. These are **Coltrane changes**, named after John Coltrane, who built tunes such as Giant Steps on them.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S3">
Now from C: the ii–V–I you have played since the start of the jazz track, sent on a detour through two other keys before it gets home.

<Editor exercise="detour-through-e" />

<OnPass>Dm7, E♭7, A♭maj7, B7, Emaj7, G7, Cmaj7: three homes a major 3rd apart, A♭, E and C, each reached by its own V7. The ii–V–I is still there at both ends; the middle travels.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S4">
The guide-tone thread from your first jazz tunes returns, now through three keys in eight bars. The bars loop over the drums, and the tenor is waiting for a line.

<Make exercise="your-line-through-three-keys" />

<CanDo id="cd.code.coltrane-changes" />

You can now play through Coltrane changes: a line that finds the 3rd or 7th of each new key as it arrives. Next: the jazz checkpoint, which opens your rhythm-changes contrafact and your reharmonized standard.

<Recap />
</Step>
