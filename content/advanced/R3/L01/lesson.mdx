<Step id="S1">
One groove, two bass lines. Drums and chords stay put; only the bass changes, and with it the whole groove.

<Example id="groove" views="code,roll" autoplay />
</Step>

<Step id="S2">
Feel it before you write it. Nod on the beat with the kick and the snare, and let your taps follow the bass.

<Tap exercise="tap-the-bass" />

<OnPass>Three taps a bar: on beat 1, between beats 2 and 3, and on beat 4. The one between the beats is what makes each bar lean into the next.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S3">
Now write what you tapped. Eight slots, one per eighth note: put the bass's three notes where they belong.

<Editor exercise="three-three-two" />

<OnPass>Three, three, two: three notes across the eight eighth notes of a bar. That is the **tresillo**, the bass rhythm under habanera, son, rock and roll and a great deal of today's pop. A good place to stop for today if you need to.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S4">
Strudel has a shorter way to ask for the same thing: a number of hits and a number of steps, in brackets after the sound.

<Predict exercise="three-over-eight" />
</Step>

<Step id="S5">
`(3,8)` is the tresillo you wrote by hand. The two numbers are yours to change.

<Editor exercise="five-over-eight" />

<OnPass>`(5,8)`: the tresillo's three hits, with one more just before the second and the third. That is the **cinquillo**, the rhythm at the heart of the Cuban danzón, and it still leans the way the tresillo does.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S6">
By ear now. The kick and the hats are your ruler; count the rimshots against them.

<Choice exercise="three-five-or-four" />

<OnPass>Three uneven hits is the tresillo, five is the cinquillo, four even ones on the beat is straight. The kick is the same in every groove; only the spacing changes what you feel.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S7">
The band plays drums and two chords. The bass and the cowbell are yours: put the bass on the tresillo, then make the groove your own.

<Make exercise="your-tresillo-groove" />
