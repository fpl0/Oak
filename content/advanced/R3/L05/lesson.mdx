<Step id="S1">
Any groove you love on a record is a stack of lines like these, and you can take each one down by ear. Today is a lab: the tape is a son groove like the one in the last lesson; you will name what you hear and take parts of it down, one line at a time.

<Example id="tape" views="code,roll" autoplay />
</Step>

<Step id="S2">
Four rhythms from the whole track. Each has a ruler to count against: the cowbell, the hi-hat, the kick.

<Choice exercise="name-the-rhythm" />

<OnPass>The clave by the bar that holds three, the seven by where its long group falls, three against two by how often the two pulses meet, the swing by the length of the pair. Every one of them is a count against something steady.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S3">
Now write one down. Count the bass against the cowbell's beats and the eighths between them.

<Editor exercise="take-the-bass-down" />

<OnPass>Two notes a bar, neither on beat 1: the tumbao, taken down by ear. With the right slots the given notes fall into place, and your bass is the tape's. A good place to stop for today if you need to.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S4">
The tape has changed one line. Take down the new cowbell.

<Editor exercise="take-the-bell-down" />

<OnPass>The cinquillo's five hits, each one an eighth later: the same figure, rotated by a step. It no longer marks beat 1, so the groove leans further forward.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S5">
The figure you just heard moved is the oldest trick in the track: state it, then bring it back displaced. Here the cowbell does it while the rest of the tape holds still.

<Make exercise="call-and-displaced-answer" />

<CanDo id="cd.transcribe.rhythms" />

You can now take down syncopated, swung and odd-meter rhythms by ear, by counting them against the line that keeps time. Next: the rhythm checkpoint, then two capstones, a sixteenth-note funk pocket with horn stabs and a son groove of your own.

</Step>
