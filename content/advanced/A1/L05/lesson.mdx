<Step id="S1">

<Step id="S2">
Push a sound harder than it can go and it changes character. Before you run it, pick what four bars of more and more pushing will do to the bass. <CodeRef fn="distort" />

<Predict exercise="push-the-bass" />
</Step>

<Step id="S3">
The scope shows what pushing does to the shape of a wave. A sine is the plainest shape there is.

<Example id="flat-tops" views="code" />

<OnPass>At 3 the round hill is squashed flat, close to a square, and it buzzes like one. A new shape is a new set of harmonics. Pushing a sound until its peaks round off and flatten is **saturation**: a little makes a part warm and thick, a lot makes it harsh, and either way it gets louder, so turn a saturated part down before you judge it.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S4">
Match a warm bass by ear. Compare at the same loudness: louder always sounds better at first.

<Editor exercise="warm-the-bass" />

<OnPass>A bass with a growl under the riff, still in proportion. On small speakers that growl carries the bass line further, because the new harmonics sit where small speakers can play them. A good place to stop for today if you need to.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S5">
The other way to damage a sound is to make it coarser. <CodeRef fn="crush" />

<Choice exercise="fewer-bits" />

<OnPass>Sixteen bits is too fine to hear; at four, every point of the wave snaps to one of a few steps and a hard grain rides on each note. An effect that rebuilds a sound from fewer steps of level is a **bitcrusher**: each bit fewer halves the steps. `.coarse()` does the same to time, holding each point of the wave for a few more.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S6">
Two kinds of grit, and one change that is not grit at all. Listen to the riff the second time round.

<Choice exercise="which-grit" />

<OnPass>A bitcrusher's grain is a hard buzz riding on top of the note; saturation thickens the note itself and pushes it forward. Oak brings sounds like these back in your warm-ups.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S7">
Back to the clean loop, or a loop of your own. Choose where the dirt goes and how much, by ear, and keep every part where it sat before.

<Make exercise="dirty-it-up" />

You can now give any part warmth or grit, with saturation or a bitcrusher, and keep it in proportion. Next in this track: a slow wave that makes a sound move while it plays, a singer's wobble and a pad that breathes.
