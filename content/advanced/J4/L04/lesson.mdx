<Step id="S1">
This is how a standard reaches a jazz player: one page, a melody on the staff and chord symbols above it. Read the page and you can play the tune. The tune on this page is Oak's, written in the style of the songs of 1930, over chords you already know.

<LeadSheet example="the-page" />

<OnPass>The chords are the A section of I Got Rhythm, the eight bars you put together last lesson. The page sets a new tune on top of them, and it carries both: the melody on the staff, the chords in the symbols above.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S2">
Read the page closely, one bar at a time.

<LeadSheet example="read-the-page" />

<OnPass>The first phrase ends in bar 4, and every bar starts on a note of its chord, five of the eight on its 3rd. The melody outlines the changes, with steps and passing notes in between. A jazz tune's written melody is its **head**: the band plays it at the start and at the end, and the solos go over its changes in between. A new head written over the changes of another tune is a **contrafact**: the chords are borrowed, the melody is new. Most bebop tunes on rhythm changes are contrafacts of I Got Rhythm.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S3">
Play from the page. No code for the head and no roll: only the lead sheet.

<LeadSheet example="the-first-two-bars" />

<Editor exercise="play-from-the-page" />

<OnPass>D held for two beats, then B♭ and D; E♭ and A, two beats each. The swing is in the playing, not on the page: eighths are written even and played long–short.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S4">
A wrong chord under a right melody can hide on paper. The ear finds it.

<LeadSheet example="four-bars" />

<Editor exercise="chords-from-the-symbols" />

<OnPass>Gm7 and Cm7, as printed: the same roots, minor sevenths instead of dominants. With the wrong two, the melody's B♭ sat against a B natural in bar 1 and its E♭ against an E in bar 2.</OnPass>
</Step>
