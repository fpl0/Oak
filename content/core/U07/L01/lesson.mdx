<Step id="S2">

<Step id="S3">
Back to the hook. The roll puts every note at its height, so the line you saw is there to measure.

<Example id="find-the-top" views="code,roll" />

<OnPass>The tune climbs to A in bar 2, dips back to D in bar 4, climbs again, reaches F5 once, on beat 1 of bar 6, and walks home from there. Most of its moves are steps or small skips of a 3rd; one big leap, up a 6th from A to that F, takes it to the top. The up-and-down shape of a melody is its **contour**.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S4">
Every note in this line belongs to its chord, and it still sounds like a box of parts. The problem is its contour: it jumps from low to high and back with nothing between.

<Editor exercise="tame-the-line" />

<OnPass>Now the line climbs from home to degree 6 (A) by step and turns back. Inside an octave and a 3rd a voice can sing all of it, and a listener can follow it.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S5">
A tune spends its high note once. Where it spends it changes what the whole tune feels like.

<Choice exercise="where-is-the-top" />

<OnPass>With its high note in bar 6 the tune keeps building for most of its length, then lets go. That high point, reached once and on purpose, is the tune's **climax**. Most songs put it in the second half.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S6">
Ears only now. Four tunes, four contours.

<Choice exercise="hear-the-shape" />

<OnPass>Climb, fall, arch, valley: most tunes are made of these four, one phrase at a time.</OnPass>
</Step>
