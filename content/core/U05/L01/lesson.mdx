<Step id="S3">
Four stacks, one a bar, over the band's bass. Run them and read the labels.

<Example id="the-loop" views="code,roll" />

<OnPass>Every stack skips a degree, then another: three notes a 3rd apart. Three notes stacked in 3rds make a **triad**, and you can stack one on any degree.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S4">
The bass in bar 3 plays degree 6. Its stack is still a single note.

<Editor exercise="build-bar-three" />

<OnPass>`[5,7,9]`: A, C and E. Each triad here sits on the note the bass plays, and is named after that bottom note, the note it is built on: its **root**. The bass plays the roots; the stack says what kind of chord sits on each.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S5">
The same stacking works on the keyboard. On white keys a 3rd always skips one key.

<OnPass>F, A, C: skip a key, skip a key. The triad on degree 4, with F as its root.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S6">
On the staff a triad looks like a snowman: three notes on three lines in a row, or in three spaces in a row. Skipping a letter each time is what a 3rd looks like on paper, and the numbers under the notes are the degrees you stacked.

<Staff example="on-the-page" />
</Step>

<Step id="S7">
Not every three notes at once is a triad. Notes right next to each other rub; notes a 3rd apart settle.

<Choice exercise="stack-or-cluster" />

<OnPass>The stack sounds open and at rest. The cluster is three neighbours fighting for the same space.</OnPass>
</Step>
