<Step id="S1">
Four chords, C F G C, over a bass that plays their roots. You will hear them twice: once as you have stacked chords so far, then played the way a pianist's hand would.

<Example id="hook" views="code,roll" autoplay />
</Step>

<Step id="S2">
Same chords, same bass, two ways of placing the notes.

<Choice exercise="one-hand" />

<OnPass>In card A the chords move like one hand that hardly leaves its place. In card B the hand jumps to a new spot for every chord.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S3">
Here is the first change of the gliding version on its own. The note lists say exactly where each note sits.

<Example id="c-to-f" views="code,roll,staff" />

<OnPass>C sits in both chords, so it stays put, and E and G each step up to their neighbour: a half step to F, a whole step to A. A note two neighbouring chords share is a **common tone**.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S4">
The chord on degree 5 shares one note with C. Hold it, and move the other two as little as you can.

<Editor exercise="c-to-g" />

<OnPass>C E G to B D G: G holds, E falls a whole step to D, C a half step to B. Moving each note of a chord to the nearest note of the next is **voice leading**. Each line of notes, bottom, middle and top, is a voice.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S5">
The whole progression now, over the same bass. The bass can leap; the three notes above it should not. F and G share no note, so there every voice has to move, and one of them may need a 3rd. This is the check Oak uses before it hands chord names over to Strudel: hints 1 to 3 are fine, and you can try as often as you like.

<Editor exercise="hand-voiced-cadence" />

<OnPass>Three voices and no leaps: C holds into F, every voice moves a short way into G, and G holds into the last C. That is how a keyboard player's hand moves through these chords.</OnPass>
</Step>
