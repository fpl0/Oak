<Step id="S3">
Here are both changes on their own, stacked by hand so the roll shows each note.

<Example id="two-pairs" views="code,roll" />

<OnPass>A C E became A C♯ E, and D F A became D F♯ A: one note up a half step each time. The minor key on A borrows from the major key on A, the trade of the sad IV, run the other way. Ending a minor piece on the major home chord is called a **Picardy third**, after the raised 3rd that makes it. The bright IV is the chord you met in Dorian.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S4">
Now in code, in another minor key. Play the target first, then change two chords.

<Editor exercise="brighten-two-chords" />

<OnPass>Em, A, B, E: i, IV, V, I. Two 3rds rose, G to G♯ in the last chord and C to C♯ in the second, and the phrase now ends in the light. A good place to stop for today if you need to.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S5">
In a minor key the numerals count against the minor scale, as they did in the minor unit. A borrowed major chord keeps its number and changes its case.

<OnPass>i IV V I. Degree 4 and degree 1 each appear in upper case, where D minor's own chords are lower case: both are borrowed from D major.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S6">
Your voice is analysed live on this computer. Nothing is recorded or sent.

The two endings differ in one sung note. Down the minor home chord it is sol, me, do; down the major one, sol, mi, do.

<Sing exercise="me-then-mi" />

<OnPass>Me, then mi: ♭3, then 3, one half step apart over the same home note. That half step is the whole of the Picardy third.</OnPass>
</Step>
