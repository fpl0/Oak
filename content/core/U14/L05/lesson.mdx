<Step id="S3">
The chord under the riff moved too. Here is the chord on degree 2 on both scales, stacked from its root.

<Example id="degree-two" views="code,roll" />

<OnPass>F♯ became F, and the diminished chord on degree 2 turned into F major. A minor scale with its 2nd lowered a half step is **Phrygian**. Its 2nd sits a half step above home and presses down on it: the leading tone of major pulls up into home from below; this note falls into it from above.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S4">
The numerals of the loop. Minor-side modes are written as minor is, so the home chord is i.

<OnPass>i ♭II i ♭II. The ♭ says the root sits a half step lower than minor's own 2nd; upper case says the chord on it is major. Two chords a half step apart, and the top one falls back onto home every time. A good place to stop for today if you need to.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S5">
Back to the riff, in E minor over one chord. Play the target first.

<Editor exercise="give-it-the-edge" />

<OnPass>One word and one chord: the riff's F♯s became Fs, and F major sits under the second bar. The F in the riff and the F chord under it press on the E that follows.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S6">
With Phrygian you have all six colours: three on the major side, three on the minor side. Each one is a scale you know with at most one note changed.

<Choice exercise="all-six-colours" />

<OnPass>Home, then the 3rd, then the one note. That order narrows six colours to two, and then to one.</OnPass>
</Step>
