<Step id="S2">
A minor has the same seven notes as C major, with a new home. Think about which notes C, E and G are before you answer.

<Predict exercise="the-chord-on-flat-3" />
</Step>

<Step id="S3">
The chord band names each chord as it sounds. Watch it, and listen to the colour of each chord against home.

<Example id="the-seven" views="code,roll" />

<OnPass>i ii° III iv v VI VII. The chords on 1, 4 and 5 are minor, ii° is diminished, and the chords on ♭3, ♭6 and ♭7 are major: the bright corners of a dark key. The major chord on ♭7, G, sits a whole step below home. That note and its chord are the **subtonic**.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S4">
A loop that never leaves the dark. Give its second half two of the key's major chords.

<Editor exercise="lift-the-second-half" />

<OnPass>Am Dm G C: i iv VII III. The loop climbs out of the dark in bar 3 and stays bright on III in bar 4, before i pulls it back. You have the groove from the start of the lesson in your own hands, a good place to stop for today if you need to.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S5">
Chord names on a page, numerals in your head. Read each chord against A, not against C.

<OnPass>i VI VII i. The same F and G that are IV and V in C major are VI and VII here, because home is A.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S6">
The bass tells you the loop. Under a minor loop it often stands on me, le and te, the roots of the key's major chords.

<EarDrill family="fam.har.bass" level={5} trials={4} />

<OnPass>The bass under a minor loop, by ear. These loops come back in your warm-up.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S7">
