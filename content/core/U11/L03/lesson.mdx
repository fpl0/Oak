<Step id="S3">
The whole scale, up to the octave and back, over the vamp. Read the labels as it plays.

<Example id="six-notes" views="code,roll" />

<OnPass>1 ♭3 4 ♭5 5 ♭7, sung do me fa se sol te: the minor pentatonic with ♭5 squeezed in between 4 and 5. That is the **blues scale**. Its three flattened notes, ♭3, ♭5 and ♭7, played over major chords like C and F, are the **blue notes**: a minor sound against a major one, the rub at the heart of the blues.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S4">
A blue note does not have to stay put. Played just before the note above it, it sounds like a guitarist bending a string up into place.

<Editor exercise="bend-into-the-3rd" />

<OnPass>E♭ rising into E: the blue 3rd bending into the chord's own 3rd. One note name, and the lick has the blues in it. A good place to stop for today if you need to.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S5">
Three scales now share home and most of their notes. Tell them apart by the 3rd, and by what sits between 4 and 5.

<Choice exercise="which-scale" />

<OnPass>Sweet means 3: the major pentatonic. A biting ♭3 means one of the other two, and a note slipping between 4 and 5 makes it the blues scale.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S6">
The same blue notes can also be written as steps of C major with a flat after them: `n("2b")` is ♭3: `n` 2, which is degree 3, then the flat. This code plays C E♭ E G♭ G B♭. You will write steps like these later in the course; for now, read it and press Play.

<Example id="altered-degrees" views="code,roll" />
</Step>
