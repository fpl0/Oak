<Step id="S3">
Run the second tune again and read the degree labels as it plays.

<Example id="five-notes" views="code,roll" />

<OnPass>Degrees 1, 2, 3, 5 and 6: the major scale without 4 and 7, the two notes that sit a half step from a neighbour (4 above 3, 7 below 1). A five-note scale like this is a **pentatonic scale**, and this one is the major pentatonic. With the half steps gone, the worst any note does over the loop is add a colour, like E over F; nothing grinds the way F did over C. In code `n` now counts the five notes, so `n` 3 is degree 5 and `n` 4 is degree 6.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S4">
A line moves from the major scale to the pentatonic. The scale word changed; the numbers have to follow.

<Editor exercise="same-notes-new-numbers" />

<OnPass>C D E G, A G E, now as `n` 0 1 2 3 and 4 3 2: the same notes, counted on five. A good place to stop for today if you need to.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S5">
Seven notes and five sound different, even as a quick run. Listen for the half steps.

<Choice exercise="five-or-seven" />

<OnPass>The major scale walks in small steps, two of them half steps. The pentatonic leaps over 4 and 7, so it has two wider gaps, between 3 and 5 and between 6 and the top 1. That open sound is the one you hear in folk tunes, soul hooks and guitar solos.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S6">
The same five degrees work from any home. Build them from G.

<OnPass>G A B D E: degrees 1 2 3 5 and 6 of G major, with C (degree 4) and F♯ (degree 7) left out.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S7">
Five notes, and no wrong ones over this loop. Move the tune onto them, then change anything: any number you pick will fit. This is improvising, one edit at a time.
