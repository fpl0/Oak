<Step id="S1">
Blues players talk to each other. One plays a short riff and leaves room; another fills the room. W. C. Handy's St. Louis Blues (1914) is built the same way: each line is sung, then the band answers.

<Example id="hook" views="code,roll" autoplay />
</Step>

<Step id="S2">
Read the organ's line before you hear it.

<Predict exercise="when-does-the-organ-play" />
</Step>

<Step id="S3">
Run it with the chord band open and keep an eye on the guitar as the chords move under it.

<Example id="riff-stays" views="code,roll" />

<OnPass>The riff never changes, while the chords go from C7 to F7 to G7 under it: that is how a blues riff works. It comes round three times, once a line, and each time the organ answers.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S4">
The guitar calls; the organ is waiting. Its answer starts higher, takes the call's notes somewhere else, and comes home.

<Editor exercise="answer-the-call" />

<OnPass>Call, answer, three times over the twelve bars: a whole chorus of blues conversation from one riff and one reply. A good place to stop for today if you need to.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S5">
In a sung blues the singer takes the first part of each line and the band answers in the rest. Hear where one hands over to the other.

<Choice exercise="where-the-answer-starts" />

<OnPass>The tune stops halfway through bar 3, and the organ answers from there to the end of the line. Singers leave that room on purpose: the answer is part of the song.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S6">
Your voice is analysed live on this computer. Nothing is recorded or sent.

Singing a riff back is how players learn them, and singing an answer is how they find their own.

<Sing exercise="sing-the-call-back" />

<OnPass>Do, me, fa, sol: the riff's first bar, with the blue third in it.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S7">
The organ already answers. Give it something to answer: one riff, the same in every line, then make both parts your own.

<Make exercise="riff-and-response" />

</Step>
