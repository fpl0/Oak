<Step id="S1">
You know this moment from records: the last line before a chorus, where the harmony seems to tilt forward and the next chord lands harder than it should. Here it is twice. The first time the turn stays inside the key. The second time one note is raised.

<Example id="hook" views="code,roll" autoplay />
</Step>

<Step id="S2">
Yesterday you sang notes from outside the key and felt each one lean. Today one of them gets a chord under it. Guess which note before you hear it.

<Predict exercise="which-note-moved" />
</Step>

<Step id="S3">
Two pairs of chords, stacked by hand from the root so the roll shows them bottom to top. The first pair is the strongest move a key has. The second pair is that move again, somewhere else.

<Example id="two-pairs" views="code,roll" />

<OnPass>G7 to C: the key's V7 goes home. D7 to G: the same move a 5th higher, so D7 is G's own V7. G is the V of this key, which makes D7 the V of V. A chord like that is a **secondary dominant**. It is written V7/V and said "five-seven of five". Three of its notes are the key's own; the fourth is F♯, yesterday's fi, G's leading tone on loan.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S4">
Before the name, there was the sound: one bar that leans. Find it with your ears, in two keys.

<Choice exercise="find-the-lean" />

<OnPass>Bar 3 in the first loop, bar 2 in the second: each time the bar right before V. That is where a V7 of V lives, and why V sounds so sure of itself afterwards. A good place to stop for today if you need to.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S5">
Now build one in a key where you have not seen it. Play the target first, then change bar 3.

<Editor exercise="lean-into-c" />

<OnPass>`G7`: G, B, D and F. In F major the key's own chord on G is minor, with B♭. Raising that B♭ to B gives C its leading tone, and bar 3 now pulls into bar 4 the way C7 pulls home to F.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S6">
The chord has two roots worth knowing: the note it is built on, and the chord it is aimed at. Its numeral names the second.

<OnPass>IV, V7/V, V7, I. D7 stands on degree 2, but the key's own chord there is D minor. The numeral says what the chord does instead: it is the V7 of V, and the slash reads "of".</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S7">
Your voice is analysed live on this computer. Nothing is recorded or sent.

Inside the three chords one line climbs by the smallest steps there are. Sing it and the lean is in your own throat.

<Sing exercise="sing-the-note-that-moves" />

<OnPass>Fa, fi, sol. Fa belongs to D minor, fi turns the chord into D7, and sol is where fi was always going.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S8">
The drums are in. Your verse has eight bars and ends on V without much conviction. Give that V a chord of its own in front of it, and let the tune follow the raised note.

<Make exercise="lean-into-the-last-bar" />

<Callout kind="tip">The V of V does not need a whole bar. `[D7 G7]` puts both chords in one bar, two beats each, and the lean gets quicker.</Callout>

<CanDo id="cd.write.secondary-dominant" />

You can now build the V7 of V, hear the bar where it leans, and name it. Next: the same lean aimed at three other chords of the key, inside a soul ballad.

</Step>
