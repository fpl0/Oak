<Step id="S2">
The roll and the keyboard show the same notes two ways. Every label on the roll is a key you can point at.

<OnPass>That is `e4`: two white keys up from `c4`, with `d4` between them.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S3">
The white keys use seven letters: `c d e f g a b`. After `b` the letters start again at `c`, one octave higher.

The number after the letter is that octave. It changes at every `c`, never in between: `b3` is the white key just below `c4`, and `c5` follows `b4`.

To find a `c` on any keyboard, look for a pair of black keys: the white key just left of the pair is a `c`. `c4` is the one closest to the middle of the keyboard. Oak also gives every note a number, and `c4` is 60.

<Example id="white-keys" views="code,roll" />
</Step>

<Step id="S4">
No code this time: your mouse or your keys do the playing.

<OnPass>`c4`, the `c` in the middle of the keyboard, is **middle C**. Its number is 60, and every other note is counted from it.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S5">
Play from the roll: read each label, then write it down.

<Editor exercise="write-the-roll" />

<OnPass>`e4 g4 a4 g4`: four letters, each with its number. Leave a number off and Strudel picks octave 3 for you, which is why Oak always asks for one.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S6">
Each octave is twelve numbers wide.

<Choice exercise="number-of-c3" />

<OnPass>48: twelve below 60. One octave down, twelve numbers down.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S7">

Some settle more than others: `c`, `e` and `g` rest on the first chord, `f`, `a` and `c` on the second. Pick at least four letters and give the riff a shape: a climb, a fall, or a jump and a return.
