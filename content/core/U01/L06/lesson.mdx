<Step id="S1">
The hats are busier than anything you have written so far, and the toms tumble down at the end of every bar. The beat underneath has not changed: still four to a bar.

<Example id="hook" views="code,roll" autoplay />

<Callout kind="tip">Drummers count this out loud as "one-e-and-a, two-e-and-a". The words keep your place when the sounds come quickly.</Callout>
</Step>

<Step id="S2">
Same backbeat as before. Only the hats line is new.

<Predict exercise="count-the-hats" />
</Step>

<Step id="S3">
Every beat now holds four hats, and each gets a word: the beat's number, then e, then &, then a. Beside the roll, the same bar is written out the way a drummer reads it.

<Example id="count-it" views="code,roll" />

<OnPass>That hat is the e of 3. Each hat in this bar is a **sixteenth note**: a quarter of a beat, four to a beat, sixteen to a bar. It is half an eighth note, the way an eighth is half a quarter.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S4">
Quarter-note hats are steady but stiff. Make them run.

<Editor exercise="sixteen-hats" />

<OnPass>Sixteen hats, four on every beat. `[hh hh hh hh]*4` writes the same bar: one beat's worth, four times over.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S5">
Toms are the drums a drummer rolls across at the end of a bar: `ht` high, `mt` mid, `lt` low. A quick run of them on the last beat pulls the ear into the next bar. `!` repeats a sound as its own step, so `ht!2` is `ht ht`.

<Editor exercise="tom-run" />

<OnPass>Two high, two mid, all inside beat 4: 4, e, &, a. Without the brackets each tom would take a slot of its own, and the bar would hold seven slots instead of four.</OnPass>
</Step>

<Step id="S6">
Now by ear and by hand. One bar plays; tap it back. The busy beat is where the counting words help most.

<Tap exercise="echo-sixteenths" />
</Step>

<Step id="S7">
Your turn. Start from an eighth-note groove and give beat 1 the full count. Toms, `!` and a second kit are all welcome.

<Make exercise="busy-groove" />

</Step>
