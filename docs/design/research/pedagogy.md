## Established curricula: Open Music Theory v2 — order of topics [verified-in-source]
Fundamentals, in this order: notation → clefs → keyboard & grand staff → half/whole steps & accidentals → American Standard Pitch Notation (e.g. C4) → rhythm notation → simple meter → compound meter → major scales/degrees/key signatures → minor scales → intro to modes & chromatic scale → sight-singing & dictation basics → intervals → triads → seventh chords → inversion → Roman numerals → figured bass → SATB. Diatonic harmony is taught through the phrase model (T–PD–D–T): cadences → V7 → strong predominants → embellishing tones → cadential 6/4 → … → tonicization → modulation. Chromatic: modal mixture → Neapolitan → augmented sixths → … Jazz unit: Swing Rhythms → Chord Symbols → Jazz Voicings → ii–V–I → Embellishing Chords → Substitutions → Chord-Scale Theory → Blues Harmony → Blues Melodies & Blues Scale. Popular-music unit: Rhythm & Meter in Pop → Drumbeats → Melody & Phrasing → Form (AABA/Strophic, Verse-Chorus) → Texture → Harmonic Schemas (Blues-Based, Four-Chord, Classical-in-pop, Puff, Modal) → Pentatonic Harmony → Fragile/Absent/Emergent Tonics. Also has Hypermeter, Metrical Dissonance and Orchestration chapters. Takeaway: reuse OMT's pop 'schemas' framing for progressions, its jazz unit order, and its phrase-model approach to function. Skip species counterpoint, galant schemas, SATB and set theory: they don't serve the goals.

## Strudel features that matter for pedagogy (toolbox mapping) and traps [verified-in-source]
Verified in the tonal docs: `n("0 2 4 6 4 2").scale("C:major")`. The scale index starts at 0, so degree 1 = n 0. Teach this explicitly as 'array index = degree − 1' and show 1-based degree labels in the piano roll and staff. Also verified: `transpose(semitones)`, `scaleTranspose(steps)`, `rootNotes(octave=2)` (unlock with bass lines), and `chord("<C Am F G>").voicing()` with controls `dict`, `anchor` (default c5), `mode` (below|above|duck|root, e.g. "root:g2"), `offset`, and `n` (index into the voicing). Chord symbols follow iReal conventions: `-` = m, `^` = maj7, `+` = aug. Trap: `voicing()` does voice leading automatically and hides inversions, so lock it until the learner has voiced inversions by hand with note lists. The docs include a jazz-blues-in-F example where one `chord()` pattern drives melody, comping and bass with `.set(chords)`; it is ideal for J2. `swingBy(x, n)` splits each cycle into n slices and delays the second half of each slice by x relative to the half-slice. `swing(n)` = `swingBy(1/3, n)`, so with 1 cycle = one 4/4 bar, `swingBy(1/3,4)` gives triplet-swung 8ths. `pattern.queryArc(begin, end)` and `firstCycle()` return the events (haps) for automatic grading. Convention trap: Strudel has no built-in meter (a cycle is not necessarily a bar). Lessons should enforce 1 cycle = 1 bar and setcpm(bpm/beatsPerBar).

## Pitch labels and solfège choice [documented]
Primary label: scale-degree numbers relative to the tonic with accidentals (1 2 b3 4 5 b6 b7, #4 …). They line up with Roman numerals (bVII, bVI), Nashville numbers, modes, borrowed chords and jazz chord tones. Sung aid: movable-do with do-based minor (do re me fa sol le te), because one-syllable names are easier to sing than 'flat three'. Evidence on fixed vs movable do is mixed. Hung 2012 (85 college music majors) favored fixed-do for sight-singing accuracy. Older work (Buchanan 1946) and a child study (Holmes 2009) favored movable-do for amateurs and beginners. Choir experience predicted success better than the system did. Berklee's ET core uses movable-do. For an adult whose goals are functional (Roman-numeral transcription, composing), relative labels win on fit with the goals rather than on head-to-head evidence.
  src: https://repository.usfca.edu/diss/38 (Hung 2012) ; journals.ou.edu/jmtp (Larson on minor-mode solfège) ; https://college.berklee.edu/node/2464596

## Ear training: tonal context first (Functional Ear Trainer method) [documented]
FET (Alain Benbassat): the user picks a key, the app plays a cadence to establish it, then a random note; the user names the scale degree. The goal is to learn each degree's 'personality' (scale-degree qualia) within a key, starting with a few degrees and expanding; sessions of about 10 min a day are recommended. Recommended Oak drill: I–IV–V–I cadence (vary voicing and instrument sound) → target note → answer → app plays the melodic resolution to the tonic and the learner SINGS it (graded by mic). Introduce degrees as 1/3/5 → +2/4 → +6/7 → minor b3/b6/b7 → chromatic. Change the key every question so the learner cannot rely on absolute pitch. Theory support: tonal hierarchy / key profiles (Krumhansl & Kessler 1982, Psychological Review 89(4)); scale-degree qualia and expectation (Huron, Sweet Anticipation, 2006). Known failure: users report 95–100% in-app scores yet weak ability with real music, so add tasks on real music (folk tunes, standards, the learner's own songs) and vary instrument sound, register, tempo and density.

## Interval recognition: pros, cons and evidence [documented]
Pros: needed to name and notate, to spell chords and to hear leaps in melodies; context-free, so useful for jazz and chromatic lines. Cons: isolated intervals do not transfer well to hearing in a key; reference-song crutches ('Here Comes the Bride' = P4) are slow to use in real time. Evidence: Wong, Chen & Lim 2021 (Psychology of Music, doi 10.1177/0305735620922595): with reference songs and singing as aids, interleaved and blocked practice performed about the same; without aids, interleaving robustly beat blocking, and most learners wrongly judged blocking as more effective. Little, Cheng & Wright 2019 (Atten Percept Psychophys 81:344–357): continuous practice alone produced NO learning in interval comparison, but alternating practice with passive exposure did, and the gain carried over to an untrained interval-identification task. Recommendation: teach intervals as degree pairs in a key; keep a secondary isolated-interval deck (melodic ascending → descending → harmonic); interleave; add short passive 'listen-only' exposure blocks between drill sets.
  src: https://ink.library.smu.edu.sg/soss_research/4316 ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6384134

## Chord-quality and progression dictation: bass line first, schemas as chunks [documented]
Order for chord quality: M vs m (root position, close voicing), then dim and aug, then 7th qualities (maj7, 7, m7, m7b5, dim7), then inversions, then extensions. Randomize voicing, register and instrument sound so the learner hears quality rather than surface cues. Progression dictation protocol: (1) find and sing the tonic; (2) sing the bass line as degrees; (3) infer roots and qualities; (4) write Roman numerals; (5) check against the melody's chord tones. Evidence: 'What Are the Truly Aural Skills?' (Music Theory Online 27.2, 2021) argues Roman-numeral labelling can get in the way of perception; its pilot study suggests bass lines + schemata make harmonic dictation more perceptual. AP Music Theory guidance: start with the bass line only. Stevens (JMTP) warns that inferring chords from both outer voices breaks down when those voices are notated wrong, so grade the bass step separately to catch errors early. Teach named schemas (OMT: Four-Chord, Puff, Blues-based, Modal; I–V–vi–IV; ii–V–I) as whole chunks. Scaffold with partially completed dictations (Baylor open ear-training text, Unit 12).

## Melodic and rhythmic dictation; difficulty modelling [documented]
Melodic dictation, following Karpinski's model (Aural Skills Acquisition, OUP 2000: hearing → short-term memory → understanding → notation): set the key and meter first; keep fragments short enough to memorize (start with 3–5 notes / 1–2 bars, stepwise, then add leaps); always sing the fragment back before writing it; limit the number of replays as a difficulty setting. Baker's 2019 LSU dissertation 'Modeling Melodic Dictation': computed melodic features approximate, but do not perfectly predict, teachers' difficulty ratings. So generate graded difficulty from features such as note count, leap size, chromaticism, rhythmic density and tonal ambiguity, then adapt it from the learner's actual success rate. Rhythm: tap or clap first (the app checks onsets via keyboard or mic), then write it in mini-notation, then pick or produce standard notation.
  src: https://repository.lsu.edu/gradschool_dissertations/4960/ ; https://davidjohnbaker1.github.io/document/computation-chapter.html ; Karpinski 2000 (cited via MTO 27.2 and JMTP literature)

## Singing: why it speeds up learning, and how to grade it [verified-in-source]
Rationale: producing pitch forces the learner to commit to a category and makes errors visible. It builds inner hearing ('audiation' in Gordon's Music Learning Theory; Kodály's 'sound before symbol'). Berklee's ET core is built on sight-singing. In Wong et al. 2021, singing plus reference songs closed the gap between practice schedules. Uses in Oak: echo-singing, singing the tonic after a cadence, singing the resolution after each FET answer, singing bass lines before writing chord dictation, sight-singing from staff/code, and humming the PRIMM 'Predict' before pressing Run. Grading: pitchy 4.1.0 (McLeod Pitch Method; `PitchDetector.forFloat32Array(n)`, `findPitch(input, sampleRate)` returns [Hz, clarity]). Pipeline: reject frames below a clarity threshold → median-filter → convert to cents relative to the target → fold octaves (different voice ranges) → segment notes by onset/stability. Tolerance starts around ±50 cents and tightens to ±25. Require headphones to stop the app's own audio leaking into the mic.
  src: unpkg.com/pitchy@4.1.0/README.md ; npm pitchy 4.1.0 ; https://college.berklee.edu/node/2464596 ; Wong et al. 2021

## What can be auto-graded, and how [verified-in-source]
Exact: theory facts (spelling, key signatures, RN ↔ chord in key; check with tonal 6.5.1); scale-degree, interval and chord-quality ID; schema ID. Rhythmic dictation: compare the learner pattern's `queryArc(0, bars)` onset set with the target, with tolerance and partial credit by onset F-measure. Melodic dictation: align the sequence of (pitch, onset) pairs, ignoring transposition (compare degrees), with edit-distance partial credit; separate pitch and rhythm scores. Harmonic dictation: score the bass line, root, quality and RN for each chord separately. Singing: cents error and timing via pitchy. Composition: rule checks on the events (ends on the tonic, cadence type at phrase ends, share of chord tones on strong beats, range/tessitura, using the required device such as a secondary dominant) plus formative feedback from Claude (comments only, never a gate). The learner's own transcriptions: no ground truth, so use A/B self-assessment. An optional rough reference: @spotify/basic-pitch 1.0.1 (audio→MIDI with ML; TF.js, heavy) for bass and melody. essentia.js 0.1.3 (last published 2022, stale) for key/chord estimates. Use both only as hints, never as answer keys.

## Learning science: retrieval, spacing, interleaving, mastery, difficulty [documented]
Retrieval practice beats restudy for long-term retention (Roediger & Karpicke 2006, Psychological Science 17(3)). Dunlosky et al. 2013 (PSPI 14(1)) rate practice testing and distributed practice 'high utility' and interleaving 'moderate'. Spacing: Cepeda et al. 2008 (Psych Sci 19(11)) found a 'temporal ridgeline': the optimal gap between sessions grows with the retention interval, but as a fraction it shrinks (about 20–40% of a 1-week retention interval, far smaller at 1 year), so there is no fixed ratio and an adaptive scheduler is justified. Interleaving: Kornell & Bjork 2008 (learning categories such as painting styles, the analogue of chord qualities and genres); Rohrer & Taylor 2007 (math); Carter & Grahn 2016 (Frontiers in Psychology, doi 10.3389/fpsyg.2016.01251: clarinetists' interleaved-practice pieces were rated higher, with better goal setting and error detection); Wong et al. 2021 (intervals). Introduce new categories blocked in a small contrast set, then interleave. Mastery learning: Bloom; Kulik, Kulik & Bangert-Drowns 1990 (RER 60(2):265–299, 108 evaluations, average effect ≈0.52 SD reported secondhand, larger for weaker students). Use gates of about 85–90% on unit checks. Optimal difficulty: Wilson et al. 2019 (Nature Communications 10:4646): about 85% accuracy is optimal for gradient-based learners; the evidence is from models, but it is a reasonable target for adaptive drills. Deliberate practice: Ericsson et al. 1993, tempered by Macnamara, Hambrick & Oswald 2014 (Psych Sci 25(8), doi 10.1177/0956797614535810: practice explains about 21% of variance in music, about 12% overall). Feedback-rich, targeted practice matters but promises no guarantees. Citations without URLs here were not re-fetched this session.

PRIMM (Predict, Run, Investigate, Modify, Make; Sentance, Waite & Kallia 2019, Computer Science Education 29(2–3), doi 10.1080/08993408.2019.1608781): 13 schools, 493 students aged 11–14, 8–12 weeks; teachers reported that all learners made progress in mixed-ability classes. ## Gamification: what supports learning vs what is shallow [documented]
Sailer & Homner 2020 (Educational Psychology Review 32) meta-analysis: small positive effects; cognitive g=.49 (k=19, N=1686), motivational g=.36, behavioral g=.25. Game fiction and social interaction moderated the behavioral effect. Motivational and behavioral effects were less stable in the most rigorous studies. Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999 (Psych Bull 125(6)): expected tangible rewards undermine intrinsic motivation; informational positive feedback does not. Self-determination theory: autonomy, competence, relatedness. Streaks: Silverman & Barasch 2023 (JCR 49(6):1095–1117, doi 10.1093/jcr/ucac029): seeing a broken streak lowers later engagement, more so when the person blames themselves; being able to repair the streak weakens the effect. Goal-gradient (Kivetz et al. 2006) and endowed progress (Nunes & Drèze 2006): effort rises as a goal gets closer, and a progress bar that starts partly filled helps. Supportive designs: (1) unlocks are REAL capabilities (e.g. `.scale()` after U3, `chord().voicing()` after hand-voiced inversions, `rootNotes` after U8, `swingBy` after the swing lesson, Euclidean rhythms after clave); (2) the skill map is shaded by FSRS recall probability, so it fades honestly and gives a real reason to come back; (3) an album/portfolio of capstones with auto-generated liner notes listing the theory used; (4) a 'can-do' ledger ('I can hear I–V–vi–IV in real songs'); (5) every session ends with a saved musical sketch; (6) a forgiving streak (count days with ≥1 review set, weekly freeze tokens, repair by doing an extra set, no shaming); (7) choice of genre flavour for each capstone (autonomy); (8) Claude as an engaged listener (relatedness). Shallow designs to avoid: XP per click or per minute, badges for time spent in the app, punishing streak loss, leaderboards (meaningless for a single user).

## Copyright-safe repertoire (US law, as of 2026-11-10) [documented]
Rule: US compositions published in 1930 or earlier are public domain (1930 works entered on 2026-01-01; 1931 works enter on 2027-01-01). Duke's 1930 list includes: I Got Rhythm, I've Got a Crush on You, But Not for Me, Embraceable You (Gershwin); Georgia on My Mind (Carmichael/Gorrell); Dream a Little Dream of Me (registered 1930); On the Sunny Side of the Street; Body and Soul; Just a Gigolo; You're Driving Me Crazy; Beyond the Blue Horizon. Recordings from 1925 entered too, including Sweet Georgia Brown (Ben Bernie), St. Louis Blues (Bessie Smith with Louis Armstrong), Fascinating Rhythm (Whiteman), Nobody Knows the Trouble I've Seen (Marian Anderson). Caveats: only the composition is free; later recordings (e.g. Ray Charles's 'Georgia', 1960) stay copyrighted. Mood Indigo has 1931 sheet music, so it is free only from 2027. US sound recordings from 1930 are free only from 2031 (except recordings in 1930 films). Other public-domain material: earlier standards (anything published ≤1930, e.g. ragtime such as Maple Leaf Rag 1899 and The Entertainer 1902; St. Louis Blues 1914; Tiger Rag 1917; Tea for Two 1924; Bye Bye Blackbird 1926; Ain't Misbehavin' and Honeysuckle Rose 1929), traditional folk songs and spirituals, and 12-bar blues forms. Oak should ship ONLY its own Strudel renditions and lead sheets, never third-party audio. Watch for copyrighted modern ARRANGEMENTS of traditional songs (e.g. the Animals' House of the Rising Sun, Simon & Garfunkel's Scarborough Fair): transcribe the traditional melody. Outside the US, terms differ (e.g. life+70 years: Ira Gershwin died 1983, so his lyrics are protected in the EU until 2053).

## Size estimate and calibration [inferred]

## Ear-training stage plan (runs alongside the theory units) [inferred]
Stage A (U1–U2): higher/lower, same/different, pitch matching, rhythmic echo, finding the tonic after a cadence. B (U3): degrees 1-3-5 → +2,4 → +6,7 in major, FET-style, answers sung. C (U4, U7): degree pairs and intervals in a key; 3–5-note melodies sung back, then written. D (U5–U6): chord quality M/m → dim/aug; bass-degree dictation on 4-chord loops; schema ID. E (U10–U12): minor degrees b3/b6/b7, the Mixolydian b7 and Dorian ♮6 sounds, 7th-chord qualities, blues forms. F (U15–U17): chromatic degrees (#4, b2, #5, b7 as V/IV), recognizing secondary dominants and borrowed chords, detecting modulations. G (jazz): ii–V–I in major and minor, guide-tone singing, extension colour (9, #11, 13, alt), turnarounds. H (rhythm): syncopation positions, swing ratio, 16th placement, clave direction, odd-meter grouping.
